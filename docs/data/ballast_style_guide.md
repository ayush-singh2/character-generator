# Ballast Books / Blue Balloon Books — House Style Guide

Distilled, model-facing copyediting rules. Source of truth for all prose the
pipeline writes or corrects. Ballast & Blue Balloon closely follow the **Chicago
Manual of Style (CMoS)**; when a case isn't covered here, default to CMoS.

Every rule below is enforceable. When correcting text, cite the rule id (e.g.
`NUM-1`) in the change report. Preserve the author's meaning, voice, dialogue,
and line breaks — change only what a rule requires.

---

## Spelling
- **SPELL-1** Use Merriam-Webster.com. If more than one spelling is listed, use the **first** listing.
- **SPELL-2** Use American English spellings — **unless the author is from outside the US** (then follow their variety consistently, e.g. "grey", "colour").

## Numbers
- **NUM-1** Spell out whole numbers **zero through one hundred**; use numerals for **101 and above**. (ninety-nine-year lease; house is 103 years old)
- **NUM-2** Ordinals follow the same rule and are **never superscripted** — put the suffix next to the numeral: *Forty-fifth*, *122nd*.
- **NUM-3** Between one and one hundred, spell out *hundred, thousand, hundred thousand, million, billion*: "three hundred people", "two hundred thousand", "three hundred million".
- **NUM-4** Use numerals for very specific numbers and for decimal millions/billions: "10,293 macchiatos", "2.3 million people".
- **NUM-5** Always spell out a number that **begins a sentence** ("Fifty people will be…"); if awkward, rephrase so it isn't first ("Of the 214 candidates, 180 had law degrees").
- **NUM-6** Consistency override: if numerals are forced elsewhere in a sentence/passage, you may put related numbers in numerals too — just be consistent.
- **NUM-7** Dimensions follow the general rule unless commonly expressed as a numeral: "seventy-five miles an hour", "five feet ten", "size 14 dress". With an abbreviation, always numeral: "75 mph", "2 g".
- **NUM-8** Write out simple fractions ("one-quarter", "three-fifths"); complex fractions as numerals ("8 ½" x 11" paper").
- **NUM-9** Percentages: numerals + the **word "percent"** ("fewer than 3 percent"). In scientific/statistical contexts use numerals + "%" ("20%–25% increase"). If it starts a sentence, spell out ("Thirty-nine percent of respondents…").
- **NUM-10** Money: either numerals + currency symbol **or** spell everything out — be consistent ("$9" or "nine dollars"). Amounts **over one hundred always in numerals**: "$125", "$7.3 billion".
- **NUM-11** Page and chapter numbers are always numerals, even below one hundred: "page 5", "Page 394".
- **NUM-12** Time of day: numerals when followed by **a.m./p.m.** ("10:30 a.m.", "6:20 p.m."); spell out otherwise ("half past ten", "five o'clock in the morning"). Write out **noon** and **midnight**.
- **NUM-13** **fewer** for countable things, **less** for uncountable: "fewer than 50 people", "less than I do".

## Dates
- **DATE-1** Write out the full month; use numerals for the day/year: "November 30", "August 12, 2014".
- **DATE-2** A day without a month is written out: "born on the thirtieth".
- **DATE-3** Close the comma after the year: "…on Dec. 13, 1989, in Reading, Pennsylvania."
- **DATE-4** Years are numerals unless they start a sentence (rewrite to avoid): "signed in 1776".
- **DATE-5** Abbreviated years use an **outward-facing apostrophe**: "the spirit of ’76" (not "‘76").

## Apostrophes & Possessives
- **APOS-1** Singular possessive = **'s**: "Erik's shoes", "Charlie's toy". (Apostrophe marks possession, not plurality.)
- **APOS-2** Plural noun ending in s = **s'**: "the states' rights", "the students' uniforms".
- **APOS-3** Any noun ending in s (common or proper) still takes **'s**: "the virus's reach", "Dickens's job", "James's peach".
- **APOS-4** If the singular form equals the plural, use apostrophe only (no s): "economics' forerunners", "politics' true meaning".
- **APOS-5** A place/organization/publication that is a plural form ending in s takes apostrophe only: "the United States' power", "Highland Hills' late mayor".

## Ellipses
- **ELL-1** Ellipses = three dots with a space between each dot **and** a space on either side: `X . . . Y`. All three dots stay on the **same line**.
- **ELL-2** Use exactly **3 dots**. If an ellipsis ends a trailing-off sentence, do **not** add a fourth period.
- **ELL-3** A question mark or exclamation mark **after** an ellipsis is acceptable: "Are you sure . . . ?"
- **ELL-4** Normalize any Unicode "…" or run of dots to the spaced three-dot form.

## Em Dash
- **EMD-1** Em dashes (—) mark emphasis, interruption, or an abrupt change of thought.
- **EMD-2** **No spaces** on either side of an em dash: "Jess thought—and who wouldn't?—that…".
- **EMD-3** Only use an em dash when no less-disruptive punctuation will do.

## En Dash
- **END-1** En dash (–) between two numbers means "up to and including / through": "pages 75–110", "2000–2009".
- **END-2** With "from…to" and "between…and", do **not** use an en dash: "from 75 to 110", "between about 150 and 200".
- **END-3** Open-ended ranges: "Jack Stag (1950–)".
- **END-4** Use an en dash (not a hyphen) to link a compound of more than two words: "post–World War II years", "country music–influenced lyrics".

## Thoughts
- **THT-1** Either put thoughts in quotes or treat them as normal text; italics for thoughts is common in fiction — if used, be **consistent throughout**.
- **THT-2** Capitalize the first word of a thought even when it begins mid-sentence: "I thought to myself, Would one cup of coffee ever be enough for me?"

## Emphasis
- **EMPH-1** Emphasize with **italics, never all caps**: "that wine was *the best* I've ever had".

## Consecutive Adjectives
- **ADJ-1** Use a comma when each adjective modifies the noun independently: "a short, tight dress".
- **ADJ-2** No comma when the first adjective modifies (second adjective + noun): "a short lace dress".
- **ADJ-3** Cheat test: if you can insert "and" between them and/or reverse them and it still reads, use the comma.

## State & City Names
- **GEO-1** Spell out the full **state** name on every occasion (no abbreviations).
- **GEO-2** For commonly abbreviated cities (LA, NYC), spell out in full on **first mention**, then the abbreviation is fine afterward.
- **GEO-3** "United States" as a **noun** may be spelled out or abbreviated **US** (no periods). As an **adjective**, always **US**: "US dollars".

## Titles of Works (movies, shows, albums, books, sites…)
- **TTL-1** Italicize titles of movies, TV shows, books, magazines, podcasts, albums, plays, musicals, concert tours, video games.
- **TTL-2** No release year after a film unless disambiguating a remade film discussed alongside others — then "(YEAR)" after the title.
- **TTL-3** Double quotation marks for song names, episode titles (TV/podcast), magazine article titles.
- **TTL-4** Band names: **not** italicized, **not** quoted.
- **TTL-5** Websites: italicize if they have a print version; don't if they don't (*Vogue*, *Us Weekly*, Refinery29, Bustle).

## College Terminology
- **COL-1** Lowercase academic departments and majors; capitalize only proper nouns: "majoring in psychology", "majoring in English literature".
- **COL-2** Graduate terms: **alumna** (F sg), **alumnae** (F pl), **alumnus** (M sg), **alumni** (M pl / mixed pl).

## Collective Nouns (companies, institutions, countries)
- **COLL-1** Treat as singular: pronoun "it", singular verb: "Glossier has a new line…", "American Airlines has hired…".

## Race & Civil-Unrest Terms
- **RACE-1** Capitalize **Black** as a racial/ethnic/cultural adjective: "Black people", "Black culture".
- **RACE-2** Avoid **brown** as a broad racial descriptor except inside a direct quotation.
- **RACE-3** "people of color" (and POC/WOC) is acceptable for broad multi-race reference, but prefer being specific about the communities.
- **RACE-4** Choose precisely among **riot / unrest / protest / demonstration / uprising / revolt** — they are not interchangeable (riot = violent chaos; unrest = milder discontent; protest/demonstration = specific actions; revolt/uprising = broader political upheaval).

## Punctuation
- **PUNC-1** Use the **Oxford comma**.
- **PUNC-2** Single quotation marks only **inside** double quotation marks.
- **PUNC-3** A "?" or "!" goes **inside** the closing quotation only when it applies to the quoted words: `Does she say, "That's so fetch"?` / `Karen said, "There's a 30 percent chance it's raining!"`
- **PUNC-4** Parentheses: if only part of a sentence is parenthetical, the period goes **outside**; if a whole sentence is parenthetical, the period goes **inside**.
- **PUNC-5** Do **not** hyphenate an **-ly adverb** to what it modifies: "hastily established" (not "hastily-established").
- **PUNC-6** Hyphenate a compound adjective only **before** the noun: "the well-behaved dog"; but "the dog was well behaved".

## Misc. Grammar
- **MISC-1** Italicize foreign words; verify accent marks.
- **MISC-2** No double spaces between sentences.
- **MISC-3** Astrology: "zodiac"/"astrology" lowercase; signs are proper nouns (Libra, Sagittarius). Plurals: Arians, Taureans, Geminis, Cancers, Leos, Virgos, Libras, Scorpios, Sagittarians, Capricorns, Aquarians, Pisceans.
- **MISC-4** After a full name is introduced, refer by first **or** last name on later mention — match the author's choice, be consistent.
- **MISC-5** "me and X" (object) vs. "X and I" (subject).
- **MISC-6** "Advance Praise" is the correct term (comes up in back-cover copy / BCC).
- **MISC-7** "and" shortened to N is written **’n’** with smart apostrophes.

## Military Terms
- **MIL-1** Capitalize titles of armies, navies, air forces, fleets, regiments, battalions, companies, corps; and well-known unofficial names ("Green Berets").
- **MIL-2** Lowercase "army"/"navy" when standing alone, used collectively/plural, or not part of an official title.
- **MIL-3** Ranks starting E/W/O take a **hyphen before the number**: "E-3", "W-5", "O-10". Other roles do not: "18A".
- **MIL-4** "Tactics" and "techniques" are different things (don't conflate).
- **MIL-5** Arabic "al-" is lowercase unless it begins a sentence ("Jaysh al-Mahdi", "al-Qaeda").
- **MIL-6** "Osama bin Laden"; if only the surname starts a sentence, "Bin Laden".
- **MIL-7** "front line" (noun) vs. "frontline" (adjective).
- **MIL-8** Canonical spellings/acronyms (use exactly): Iraqi Army; Ranger School; Special Forces; Enemy Prisoner of War (EPW) camp; Operational Detachment Alpha (ODA); non-commissioned officer (NCO); killed in action (KIA); Radwaniyah Presidential Complex (RPC); pre-deployment site survey (PDSS); forward operating base (FOB); target intelligence packet (TIP); improvised explosive devices (IEDs); Joint Special Operations Command (JSOC); positive identification (PID); Quick Reaction Force (QRF); human intelligence (HUMINT); signals intelligence (SIGINT); rocket-propelled grenade (RPG); truck/track/tank commander (TC); Kirkush Military Training Base (KMTB); forward-looking infrared (FLIR); vehicle-borne IED (VBEID); Iraqi Special Operations Forces (ISOF); after-action review (AAR); high-value individual (HVI); time-sensitive target (TST); explosively formed penetrators (EFPs); Iraqi Counter-Terrorism Force (ICTF); Concept of Operations (CONOP); Air Weapons Team (AWT); troops in contact (TIC); sensitive site exploitation (SSE); advanced International Morse Code (AIMC); persons indicted for war crimes (PIFWCs); Humvee/Humvees.

## Commonly-Used Words (canonical spellings)
- **WORD-1** Use exactly: **nerve-wracking**; **workout** (noun) / **work out** (verb); **OK**; **canceled**, **canceling**; **toward**; **gray** (American author) / **grey** (UK author); **a.k.a.**; **ASAP**; **well-being**; **stepping stone**; **front line** (noun) / **frontline** (adjective); **T-shirt**; **nonstop**; **X-ray** (noun) / **x-ray** (verb).
